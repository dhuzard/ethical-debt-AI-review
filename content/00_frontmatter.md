## Abstract

Animals are used to generate biomedical knowledge at a real and deliberate welfare cost,
yet a large and measurable fraction of the resulting data is never published, shared, or
made reusable. This review argues that wasting animal-derived data is an unpaid *ethical
debt*: because generating the data imposed a welfare cost, allowing it to become
unfindable, unusable, or unreused is itself a welfare harm, one ultimately discharged in
the further animal lives needed to regenerate what was lost. On this view, stewardship of
animal-derived data is a dimension of animal welfare and of the 3Rs — a claim we call
*data welfare* — rather than a technical afterthought.

Synthesising evidence from 1,337 unique papers, the review establishes that the debt is
real and quantifiable. The published animal record is filtered toward positive results,
weakly protected against bias, and a poor, analysis-dependent predictor of human outcomes,
while roughly half of animal experiments are never published and only about 2% of medical
papers actually share their data against the roughly 8% that declare availability. It then
weighs four mechanisms for repaying the debt: making data Findable, Accessible,
Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR); reusing curated control-animal data as virtual control
groups, which converges on roughly a quarter fewer control animals but awaits regulatory
acceptance; disciplined stewardship of the high-dimensional data that non-animal new
approach methodologies generate; and reform of the incentive and governance systems that
currently price stewardship at near-zero. A recurring finding is that enforced
requirements move behaviour where passively endorsed ones do not, though effects are
heterogeneous and field-wide sharing remains near the floor.

Methodologically, the review was produced through a staged, human-supervised protocol in
which AI-directed components searched PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and CrossRef, extracted
and independently verified evidence with per-claim DOI provenance, and drafted the text and
figures under blinded internal assessment. Throughout, method-divergent estimates are
juxtaposed rather than averaged, and field-defining figures resting on modelled or
non-transparent sources are flagged. Key questions remain open — whether FAIRification
causally increases reuse, whether regulators will accept virtual control groups, and
whether evaluation reform changes behaviour rather than metrics — but the central claim is
simple and robust: because animals paid for the data with their lives, repaying the debt
saves animals.
